ECG Landmark Bench

Colin BudriesOctober 2026

I had free credits with a service that gives access to many AI models, and I wanted to use them to see how leading closed and open-weight models handle a task I care about. Heart health matters a great deal to me. ECGs once needed a clinic; now they are built into wearables such as Whoop, and before long many people will have a daily or even continuous ECG. If people are going to ask AI models to read those recordings, it is worth knowing whether an ordinary model can do the most basic part of the job. This does not test wearable ECGs directly. It tests a simpler first step: whether a general-purpose model can precisely locate beats in a clean ECG image.

In brief

  • We asked 8 AI models to mark every heartbeat on pictures of real ECG recordings, and compared their marks with those of expert cardiologists.
  • How close is close enough. Every mark is a moment in the recording. A model's mark counts as correct if it falls within a set time of the cardiologists' mark, the allowed timing error. At 150 milliseconds (ms) a model only has to point at the right beat. At 25 ms it must place the mark very near the cardiologists' mark: within about a quarter of the width of the spike, three fifths of one small square on ECG paper, or five to six pixels in the image.
  • On these clean ten-second strips, finding the beats was not the main difficulty for the strongest models; placing them precisely was. On the 100 test records, 6 of the 15 model and input combinations missed no beat at all. XQRS, a basic open-source beat detector that reads the raw signal rather than a picture, missed 48, including every beat on the pacemaker recordings.
  • The models differ in precision. GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5, both with zoom, placed every beat within 25 ms on 99% of records each. For context, one horizontal pixel of the image is about 4.5 ms, so the best models' errors are on roughly the scale of a pixel.
  • The largest changes with a zoom tool available came for the least precise models: Grok 4.7 went from 3% to 50% of records passed at 25 ms, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash from 1% to 49%.
  • Some models fail simply by not answering in the form asked for. With zoom offered, Muse Spark Open gave no usable answer on 26% of records.
  • On clean ECG images, the strongest general-purpose models locate beats to within roughly a pixel. Their remaining failures come less from missing heartbeats than from where exactly a mark belongs, image resolution, tool use and answers in the wrong format. Whether this carries over to noisy wearable ECGs, other parts of the waveform or clinical interpretation is still an open question.

1 Why This Matters

An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart's electrical activity as a wavy line. Locating each beat is one of the foundational steps in reading it. It gives the heart rate and the time between beats, and it is the anchor for finding the start and end of the other waves, which doctors measure to check for rhythm problems and drug side effects. Software does this well when it has the raw recording. In practice, many ECGs are stored and shared only as images or paper printouts, and general-purpose AI models are increasingly asked to read them.

This benchmark tests that first, checkable step: can a model look at the picture and say where each beat is? It does not ask whether the heart is healthy, and nothing here is a diagnosis.

2 The Task

The recordings come from the Lobachevsky University ECG Database (LUDB, version 1.0.1, published on PhysioNet): 200 people, one ten-second recording each. Two cardiologists marked every wave by eye and agreed on a single set of labels. We use one view of the heart, Lead II, and the cardiologists' annotated peak of each QRS complex, the main spike of each beat.

Each model receives one picture of the full recording on standard ECG grid paper and must reply with the times, in seconds, of every beat between 3 and 7 seconds. The experts left the first and last second or two of every recording unlabeled, so only this middle window is scored; it is shaded on the picture. In the second version of the task the model may also call a zoom tool, up to six times, to see any stretch in more detail.

Scoring. A model's mark counts as a beat found if it lies within a set time, the tolerance, of an expert mark, and each expert mark can be claimed only once. A record passes if every beat is found and no extra beats are added. We score at three tolerances, 150, 50 and 25 milliseconds; the strictest is the most informative.

A fair test. The 200 records were split at random into 100 for development and 100 for the final test. All rules were settled on the development records and then frozen. Only after that were the held-out records run, once. New models are tested under exactly the same rules.

Found, but late. With the picture alone, Grok 4.7 and Kimi K3 found most beats but placed their marks after the expert's, by 38 and 35 ms on average. Because the error is a shift in one direction rather than random scatter, nearly every record fails at 25 ms even though the beats themselves were found.

Cost and precision. Price is a poor guide to precision. Kimi K3 cost the most per record ($0.077) and passed 1% at 25 ms; GPT-5.6 Luna cost $0.002 and passed 95%. The chart below sets each model's accuracy against its cost.

Which model to use. For precision, GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 were effectively tied: both passed 99% of records at 25 ms with zoom. GPT-6.1 Sol had the lower 95th-percentile timing error, 4 ms against 6 ms, but this experiment is too small to treat that difference as meaningful. For value, GPT-5.6 Luna (image only) passed 95% of records at $0.002 each. Among the open-weight models, Muse Spark Open (image + zoom) did best, passing 74%.

Pacemaker beats expose a problem with the answer key as well as with the models. A beat driven by a pacemaker often has no upward spike. On such beats the cardiologists placed their mark partway down the slope, while every model chose either the bottom of the beat or its start, 32 to 98 ms away. The models found every one of these beats; the open-source detector found none. We have kept the experts' labels and report pacemaker records separately.

5 What Changed When Zoom Was Available?

It depends on the model. The largest observed improvements came for the two models that were least precise with the picture alone. Grok 4.7's pass rate at 25 ms rose from 3% to 50%, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash's from 1% to 49%; both zoomed five or six times on a typical record. Models that were already precise gained a few milliseconds. Claude Opus 5.5 zoomed on most records, and its 95th-percentile error fell by 4 ms (95% interval 1 to 6 ms). Claude Sonnet 5.5 almost never zoomed, so for it the zoom version of the task amounts to the same picture with a longer prompt, and the change cannot be told apart from zero. Each record was run only once in each version, so these differences include the ordinary variation between runs of the same model as well as any effect of zoom.

Muse Spark Open shows both sides. With zoom offered, its pass rate at 25 ms rose from 48% to 74%, and every answer it gave was within 25 ms. But on 26% of records it never returned a usable list of times. At the loose 150 ms tolerance, where it had passed 99% with the picture alone, those missing answers leave it worse off.

Matching. For each record, predictions and expert marks are paired one to one so as to maximise the number of pairs within the tolerance, then to minimise the total distance (scipy linear_sum_assignment, in whole microseconds). Predictions outside the 3 to 7 s window are not counted as extra beats. Answers that are not valid JSON of the form {"qrs_peaks_s": [...]}, or that contain times outside the recording, are scored as no answer.

Uncertainty. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of the 100 records. The zoom effect is a paired difference computed on the same resamples.

Models and settings. Claude models through the Anthropic API; all others through a commercial service that gives access to many models. Reasoning effort high throughout; at most 10 model turns and 6 zoom calls per record; network errors retried up to three times. The three open-weight models (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Muse Spark Open and Kimi K3) have publicly released weights but were run through hosted providers, not locally.

Limitations. The test set is small: 100 records with four or five scored beats each, and only three pacemaker records, so the intervals above are wide. Each model answered each record once; when development records were run twice, marks moved by a median of 1.5 to 10 ms, so differences of a few milliseconds between models should not be read as meaningful. LUDB has been public since 2020 and has only 200 records, so this benchmark cannot show that the models are generalizing to unseen ECGs rather than benefiting from some exposure to these recordings during training. The two cardiologists agreed on a single set of marks, so disagreement between experts cannot be measured. Settings differ slightly: DeepSeek ran without the required answer format, which it could not combine with the zoom tool, and Kimi K3 ran without the zoom tool. Clean ten-second strips from one device are easier than photographs of paper ECGs, and nothing here tests clinical use.

Records. The protocol was fixed, and its settings recorded by hash, before the test records were run. Every request, reply, zoom call and cost is logged.
